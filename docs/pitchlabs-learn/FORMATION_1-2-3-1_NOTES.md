# 1-2-3-1 (7v7): Chris's practice notes

Source material for the formations series (see PUBLISHING_CALENDAR.md, "Queue: Formations & Animate pilot"). These are Chris's own words about how he coaches the shape, captured 2026-10-09, lightly tidied. They are the only basis for first-person "I do X" claims in the articles. Anything not on this page is not yet confirmed as Chris's practice. Research is logged in COACHING_FRAMEWORK.md, not here.

## In possession, from our own goal kick

**The routine.** The central defender takes the goal kick to the goalkeeper, then checks wide. Reasoning, as stated: the keeper isn't who we want receiving the ball if the central defender is under pressure; Chris would rather the keeper be the one under pressure, with the central defender as the outlet.

**First objective.** Break the initial line of pressure with a pass or a dribble, whether one, two or more opposition players press. Stated by Chris as "the first real objective of any 7v7 formation" (wording to soften in print, per editorial principle 7: one way, not the only way).

**If the opposition doesn't press** (rare, per Chris): the central defender carries the ball and the team looks to create superiorities (numbers up).

**After the first line is broken:**
1. establish possession
2. get faced forward
3. enter the opposition half

**In the opposition half:** depending on how many players pressed our keeper, the team should be numbers up. If not, establish possession and look to create opportunities for the forward players.

## Follow-up answers, 2026-10-09

- **Routine logic confirmed:** the keeper receives first, so if anyone is pressed it is the keeper, with the central defender as the outlet, instead of a pressed defender whose only way out is the keeper.
- **Counting:** Chris does not count the keepers when counting numbers up (outfield players only). The overload is typically quite short, but should still be the goal.
- **Keeper:** at this age the keeper is rarely a full-time keeper. Teams that do have one are typically the stronger groups, where the keeper would be a decent player to begin with. The routine has risks (Chris's words), but this is why he accepts them.
- **Press timing:** the release is always either the first player passing or the second player receiving, which typically gives the keeper time. (Exact league wording still to confirm; the article should say "check your league's trigger".)

## Matchups (raised by Chris, 2026-10-09; not yet clarified)

Chris's point: how the shapes match up against specific formations matters. His first example was that the 3-2-1 is strong against a 2-3-1 "given the overload in midfield". A static line count (3 v 2 midfield, in the 2-3-1's favour) didn't match that, so it was queried and **Chris clarified the same day:** the point is about width and channels, not line counts.

**Chris's clarification (2026-10-09):**
- The 2-3-1 has two *wide* midfielders. The 3-2-1 has two *central* midfielders, which lets it control the central channel more easily.
- The difficulty for the 3-2-1 is ensuring the two wide defenders can support the attack.
- For high-level teams the 3-2-1 can look like a 1-4-1 in controlled possession (outfield notation), or even a 1-2-3, depending on how far forward the wide players get.
- His own rule of thumb: send only one wide player forward, typically on the ball side.

The line-count table drafted earlier is superseded by this and removed (it measured the wrong thing). No matchup study was found at U10 (the only 2-3-1 formation study, U19, used a 3-3 opponent), so matchup claims are Chris's coaching reasoning.

## How he coaches it (2026-10-09)

"We coach it so there's not really a set pattern, it's more about solving the problems that are presented with time, space and numbers." So the articles should not present fixed movements or a single "correct" sequence. The fixed things are the starting shape and the objectives (break the first line, establish possession, get faced forward, enter the half); the solutions depend on what the opponent does. Write "time, space and numbers" as three ordinary coaching words, not a named model (voice file: don't make everything a framework). **Confirmed 2026-10-09:** the goal-kick routine (central defender to keeper, then checks wide) is a default start that then opens into problem-solving. Chris also confirmed Animate can hold a frame, so clips can pause on the decision moment.
