# Article teasers: Instagram carousels (drafted 2026-10-21, unapproved)

Sneak-peek posts for six live Learn articles. Each one leads with the article's own finding, gives a taste of the
practical part, and sends people to the full piece. Every claim and number below is lifted from the live article
it teases. Nothing new is claimed, and the study is named on a slide the same way the article names it.

## How these work

- **Format:** carousel, 5–6 slides, 4:5 portrait (1080 × 1350). Slide 1 is the hook and has to work on its own in
  the feed. The last slide is the sign-off to the full article.
- **Look:** the Learn brand (dark green, lime accent, the display font). One idea per slide, large type, no
  paragraphs. Where a slide says "diagram", reuse the article's own image or tool screenshot.
- **Links:** Instagram doesn't make caption links clickable. Use "link in bio" in the caption, put the article in
  the bio link (or a link-in-bio page with all six), and add a link sticker when sharing the post to Stories.
- **Tracking:** use the tagged link for each post so Google Analytics shows which posts send readers:
  `https://usepitchlabs.com/learn/articles/<slug>?utm_source=instagram&utm_medium=social&utm_campaign=article_teaser&utm_content=<slug>`
- **Voice:** the account speaks as PitchLabs. Opinions stay attributed to the article ("the article's take") rather
  than written as new first-person claims.
- **Order:** suggested posting order is the order below, starting with small-sided games because the 3v3 reel
  audience is already there.

## 1. Small-Sided Games for Young Players

Slug: `small-sided-games`

**Slide 1 (hook)**
Smaller games mean more touches. Everyone knows that.
The interesting question is *whose* touches.

**Slide 2**
Ten-year-olds played 7v7, then 4v4.
The most involved third of players: +72% actions.
The least involved third: **+143%**.
*Hintermann et al., PLOS ONE, 2021*

**Slide 3**
The players who gained most from the smaller game were the ones the bigger game had been leaving out.

**Slide 4**
Small games don't give you everything.
Switching play needs a far side to switch to. Support at distance needs distance.
Some decisions need more players to exist at all.

**Slide 5**
One way to use it:
Go small for touches.
Add players when the decision needs them.
Run more games, not bigger ones.

**Slide 6 (sign-off)**
The full article: what the research on 3v3, 4v4 and 7v7 shows, what small games can't give you, and a tool to split your group.
Link in bio.

**Caption**
In one study of ten-year-olds, switching from 7v7 to 4v4 doubled the actions per player. But the bigger finding was who gained: the least involved players' actions went up 143%, twice the gain of the most involved.

The full article covers what the research shows across ages 6 to 10, what small games can't give you, and how to choose a format. Link in bio.

## 2. Why Your Soccer Practice Feels Like Chaos

Slug: `why-your-practice-feels-like-chaos`

**Slide 1 (hook)**
The same five instructions made some children better.
And others worse.

**Slide 2**
90 children aged 8 to 10 practiced basketball shooting, with five technique instructions before every block.
Higher working memory: improved steadily.
Lower working memory: got *worse*.
*Buszard et al., Frontiers in Psychology, 2017*

**Slide 3**
Every team you coach has both kinds of player.
More instruction isn't a safe default.

**Slide 4**
When a practice feels chaotic, the article's suggestion is to look at the environment before the coaching.
**S**pace. **T**ask. **E**quipment. **P**layers.

**Slide 5**
STEP turns "this isn't working" into four specific things to change, instead of one vague worry.
(STEP isn't new: the FA teaches a version of it.)

**Slide 6 (sign-off)**
The full article: how STEP works in a real session, an example game, and what to do when players can't yet do the skill.
Link in bio.

**Caption**
In one study, five technique instructions helped children with higher working memory and made children with lower working memory worse. Every group you coach has both.

When a session feels like chaos, the full article makes the case for adjusting the environment first: the space, the task, the equipment and the players. Link in bio.

## 3. The Fundamentals of Soccer Coaching

Slug: `fundamentals-of-soccer-coaching`

Images: `fundamentals-carousel/fundamentals-1.png` to `-6.png` (1080 × 1350, made 2026-10-21). The slide text in the
images is lightly tightened from the copy below; the images are the version to post.

**Slide 1 (hook)**
Drills are supposed to be best for technique.
In the head-to-head studies, they weren't.

**Slide 2**
Studies comparing game-based and drill-based practice:
Technique: most showed no difference, about a third favored games, **none favored drills**.
Tactics: games came out ahead.
*Bromilow et al., Sports Medicine – Open, 2025*

**Slide 3**
A well-run activity and good coaching aren't the same thing.
An activity can be well designed and still just be rehearsal, with no real decision in it.

**Slide 4**
Perceive. Decide. Execute. A shorthand, not three steps in a row: the research behind STEP sees perceiving and
acting as one coupled process (as the article says).
But most training time still goes to execute.

**Slide 5**
The article walks through the two tools used across PitchLabs Learn to shape the deciding: STEP and the Coaching Intervention Wheel.

**Slide 6 (sign-off)**
The full article: the fundamentals, and how they change by age.
Link in bio.

**Caption**
The surprising part of the drills-versus-games research is technique. In the studies that compared them head to head, drills didn't come out ahead even there. Most showed no difference, about a third favored game-based practice, and none favored drills.

The full article covers what that means for how you spend practice time. Link in bio.

## 4. How Much Should You Actually Say?

Slug: `how-much-should-you-actually-say`

**Slide 1 (hook)**
Your players have a better read on your coaching than you do.

**Slide 2**
Researchers compared coaches' ratings of their own behavior with what an observer recorded.
The players' ratings matched the recording more closely than the coaches' own did.
*Cushion, Ford & Williams, Journal of Sports Sciences, 2012*

**Slide 3**
Across the research on youth soccer coaching, instruction was the most common thing coaches did.
Generally more than once a minute.

**Slide 4**
Instruct is one option on a menu of twelve.
Observe. Silence. Question. Guide and discovery. And eight more.

**Slide 5**
The article's take: instructing earns its place for a skill a player has never done, and less often elsewhere.

**Slide 6 (sign-off)**
The full article: the twelve ways to interact once you've stepped in, and when each one fits.
Link in bio.

**Caption**
When researchers compared what coaches thought they did with what an observer recorded, the players' ratings matched the recording more closely than the coaches' own ratings did.

## 5. Scanning: Why Information Comes Before Decisions

Slug: `scanning-and-receiving`

**Slide 1 (hook)**
Champions League award-winners didn't scan any more than their elite teammates.

**Slide 2**
Award-winners and teammates, matched on team, match and line: no meaningful difference in how much they scanned.
*Science and Medicine in Football, 2024*
So "scan more" can't be the whole coaching point.

**Slide 3**
What the studies from U13 to U19 link to better passing is **when**: looking before the ball arrives.

**Slide 4**
A scan is quick. Elite midfielders wearing eye-tracking glasses averaged about four-tenths of a second per scan.
A glance is enough.
*Aksum et al., PLOS ONE, 2021*

**Slide 5**
Almost all of this research is on high-level players aged 13 and up. Treat it as where scanning goes, not a benchmark for 8-year-olds.

**Slide 6 (sign-off)**
The full article: what the research says, and how to make not looking cost something in the game instead of shouting "look up".
Link in bio.

**Caption**
The best players in the Champions League study didn't scan more than the very good players around them. What the youth studies link to better passing is looking before the ball arrives.

The full article covers what scanning is, what the research does and doesn't show, and how to build it into a game. Link in bio.

## 6. How to See Your Own Coaching

Slug: `see-your-own-coaching`

**Slide 1 (hook)**
Five professional youth coaches watched video of their own sessions for three seasons.
Here's what changed.

**Slide 2**
They ended up:
instructing less
giving less feedback
leaving more deliberate silence
and four of the five asked more questions.
*Partington, Cushion, Cope & Harvey, Reflective Practice, 2015*

**Slide 3**
You can't see your own coaching clearly from inside a session.
That isn't a discipline problem. Your attention is on the players, which is where it should be.

**Slide 4**
Four ways to see it:
A five-things check after training.
Ten minutes of phone video and a tally.
Asking your players.
A colleague watching.

**Slide 5**
Count a few things, not everything:
instructions, questions, silence, and every time you stop play (and who it was for).

**Slide 6 (sign-off)**
The full article: each of the four methods, step by step.
Link in bio.

**Caption**
Five professional youth coaches watched video of their own sessions over three seasons. They ended up instructing less, giving less feedback and leaving more deliberate silence, and four of the five asked more questions.
