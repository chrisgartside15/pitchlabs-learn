# Research note: sideline shouting and instruction in youth soccer (2026-10-22)

Deep dive for the "Stop Screaming 'Boot It!'" idea. Not an article, not logged in COACHING_FRAMEWORK.md yet. Tiers: **R** read at source, **A** abstract only, **S** secondary or search summary only, **X** could not be verified.

## What the evidence supports

- **Knight, Neely & Holt (2011)**, *Journal of Applied Sport Psychology* 23(1), 76–92, doi:10.1080/10413200.2010.525589. **(R, author manuscript on Swansea's Cronfa repository)**. Interviews with 36 female athletes, mean age 13.5, who played team sports, recruited at summer camps (Alberta). What they wanted from parents at games: interact positively throughout (34 of 36), keep emotions in check (36 of 36), do not coach from the sidelines (29 of 36), do not draw attention to yourself or your child (28). Athletes called parent "coaching" confusing and distracting, especially when it contradicted what the coach said. Exception: some liked one specific goal from a knowledgeable parent before or after the game, reinforcing the coach's ideas. Limits: females, about 13, one province, qualitative, no member checking. **Not youth soccer, not U9/U10.** Strongest direct support for "stop instructing from the touchline".
- **McCabe, Tsiros & Crozier (2024)**, *Frontiers in Sports and Active Living* 5:1292812, doi:10.3389/fspor.2023.1292812. **(A)**. 67 Australian youth athletes, team sports, online survey. Parent positive sideline behaviours went with the athlete's prosocial behaviours; negative with antisocial. Authors call it preliminary. Self-report, small, cross-sectional.
- **Bonavolontà et al. (2021)**, *Int J Environ Res Public Health* 18(16):8698, doi:10.3390/ijerph18168698. **(A)**. 80 Italian male soccer players. They reported excessive active involvement and pressure, and too little praise and understanding. Cross-sectional, small.
- **Buszard et al. (2017)**, already logged in COACHING_FRAMEWORK.md **(R)**: 90 children aged 8–10, five explicit instructions before each block; lower working-memory children got worse. This is coach instruction before practice shots, not shouting during a game, and not parents. Use as the cognitive-load reasoning, not as proof that sideline shouting hurts.
- **Partington & Cushion (2011)**, already logged **(R)**: instruction frequency in youth soccer coaching, and coaches are poor judges of their own frequency. Supports the coach half.

## What I could not support

- **"Children enjoyed COVID because parents weren't there."** No study found. What exists points the other way or is silent: Eime et al. (2021, *BMC Public Health*) cite research that reduced parental involvement can be read by children as less emotional support; Reynolds et al. found parents grieved the loss of spectating and reported fewer child-parent interactions; Elliott et al. (2022, *Psychology of Sport and Exercise* 64, doi:10.1016/j.psychsport.2022.102299) is parents' and 15–18 year olds' views on sport parenting during lockdown, not enjoyment without spectators. Treat the claim as anecdote.
- **Any experiment showing that shouting instructions lowers on-field performance.** None found. The evidence is children's stated preferences and correlations.
- **"Silent sideline" outcomes.** No rigorous study found. A survey of about 300 parents and coaches (D.C. Stoddert, via Slate) reports players communicating more and being less tentative; self-report, secondhand. A "87% of spectator time silent" figure attributed to Randall and McKenzie could not be traced. Do not use.
- **Unverified popular statistics** seen in search results: "17% of kids want to quit because of parents (Journal of Amateur Sport, 2025)", "3x longer enjoyment (University of Michigan)", "58% referee retention". Do not use.

## Fit with Learn's rules

- The research favours "encourage, don't instruct" for parents. For the coach audience the safer finding-led hook is the coach-side data (instruction more than once a minute, Partington & Cushion) plus the players' own preference (Knight 2011).
- Audience: parents are the sideline problem, coaches are the PitchLabs audience. Aim at coaches managing parents, or the parked parent-facing question needs a decision first.
