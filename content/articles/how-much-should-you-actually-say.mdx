You built the environment right. Space, task, equipment, players — the session is designed so the right decision is the obvious one. So why are you still talking the whole time?

Most coaches don't think of themselves as talkers. But watch the sideline at any U8 session, yours included, and count how often a coach's voice fills a gap that didn't need filling. A pass goes slightly wrong and out comes an instruction, before the player's even had a chance to notice it themselves. A game is flowing fine and it still gets stopped, because there's a coaching point sitting there, unused.

Building the right environment was half the job. This is the other half: what you actually do once the session is running — the second of the two ideas behind [The Fundamentals of Soccer Coaching](/articles/fundamentals-of-soccer-coaching).

## What coaches actually say, when someone's counting

A [season-long study of coaching behavior](https://cdn1.sportngin.com/attachments/document/feaa-2890941/A_season_long_investigation_into_coaching_behaviou.pdf) logged what three collegiate coaches (field hockey, volleyball, basketball) actually said during real sessions, not what they thought they said, over 1,000, 533 and 905 minutes of observation respectively. Instruction made up 27–37% of everything each coach said. Questioning made up only 9–14%, roughly a 3-to-1 gap in instruction's favor. Of the questions that did get asked, closed ones — the kind with one right answer the coach already has in mind — outnumbered open ones by roughly 8 to 1. One coach, asked why his questioning stayed closed, said it plainly: he "wanted the answer immediately, knew what the answer was, and knew that they could give me it."

That study is collegiate coaches, not U8, and not soccer — I haven't found the equivalent instrument-and-stopwatch version run on youth soccer coaches specifically. But the pattern is a coaching-behavior finding, not a sport-specific one: coaches default to Instruct without noticing, and closed questions crowd out open ones. Stopping to ask a real question and wait for the answer is genuinely harder than just saying the thing. It's slower in the moment, even if it teaches more over a season. Coaches default to Instruct the same way water finds the lowest point, not because anyone decided talking more was the better plan.

## Instruct is one tool, not the only one

I use a wider menu with my own coaches, and Instruct is one entry on it, not the default:

- **Observe.** Gather information before deciding whether to act at all. Says nothing yet.
- **Silence.** Withhold input on purpose, so the player has to solve it themselves.
- **Question.** An open question that makes them think — "Who was free?" — not one with a single right answer you're waiting to hear.
- **Guide and discovery.** A nudge, not the answer — "What did you see over your left shoulder?"
- **Co-create.** Build the solution with the players instead of handing it to them — "What do we want to try in the next three minutes?"
- **Check understanding.** Ask them to tell you back what they took from it.
- **Demonstrate.** Show it instead of describing it.
- **Reframe.** Change how the player sees the moment, not what they did — "That wasn't a bad pass. That was the right idea, a second late."
- **Feedback.** Tell them what happened and what it caused.
- **Reinforce.** Name precisely what was good, so it happens again.
- **Challenge.** Raise the demand on a player who's comfortable.
- **Instruct.** A direct, unambiguous command — "Body between the ball and the defender. Now." Still sometimes exactly right. Just not the only entry.

None of these are tied to a fixed situation. Experiment with your own group — the same moment might call for Silence with one player and Instruct with another, depending on what they're ready for.

## Why over-instructing doesn't just slow things down

If you read the first piece in this series, some of this will sound familiar. Ninety children were given five explicit instructions before every block of a 240-shot shooting task. The ones with higher working memory kept improving. The ones with lower working memory got measurably *worse* — the instructions themselves were the obstacle, not the skill ([Buszard et al., *Frontiers in Psychology*, 2017](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2017.01350/full)).

Every time Instruct is the reflex instead of a choice, you're adding to a working-memory load that's already close to its limit for a meaningful number of your players. Question, Silence and Guide and discovery aren't softer alternatives to Instruct. They cost the player nothing to hold in mind, because the player is doing the thinking instead of storing your words.

There's a second line of research pointing the same direction, from sport psychology rather than motor learning. A 2024 study followed 325 youth athletes, ages 12–18, across team, individual and endurance sports, and found that coaching which supported autonomy — giving athletes a real say, asking rather than dictating — predicted higher psychological resilience, which predicted more optimism, which predicted better development outcomes ([Frontiers in Psychology, 2024](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2024.1433171/full)). The sample skews older than U8 and measures resilience rather than a technical skill, but it's a second, independent line of evidence, psychological this time, that asking and involving players builds something instruction on its own doesn't.

## Should you be saying anything at all?

Everything above assumes you've already decided to step in. That's not automatic, and the two decisions get solved by different tools.

Whether to intervene at all is a STEP question, not an interaction-style one. If a session is running too easy, too difficult, too slow, or producing the wrong behavior, the first fix is the environment — space, task, equipment, players — not your voice. A coach who spots a struggling group and reaches straight for more talking is often fixing a design problem with the wrong tool. Check the environment first. If it's built right and players are still stuck, that's when you're choosing how to say something, not whether to.

Diagnose with STEP. Adjust the environment if that's the actual problem. Only then reach for one of the tools above.

## When Instruct is the right call

This isn't an argument for never instructing. Some moments need it: safety, a rule the player doesn't know yet, a skill so new there's nothing for them to discover because they don't have the reference points to discover it with. Demonstrate and Instruct do real work there that Question can't.

Ask yourself whether this is one of those moments, or whether it's just the fastest thing to say. Most of the time, honestly, it's the second one.

Here's a way to check yourself without overthinking it mid-session. After training, try to remember the last five things you said to players. If four or five of them were you telling someone what to do, that's worth noticing, not as a verdict on you as a coach but as the same kind of honest data the season-long study collected. You don't need a clipboard and a stopwatch. Most of us, like the coaches in that study, think we talk less than we do.

## Common questions about how much a coach should talk

**How much should a youth soccer coach talk during practice?**
Less than most coaches think. A season-long study of coaching behavior found coaches spend roughly three times as much talk on direct instruction as on questioning, and most of that questioning is closed rather than open, meaning coaches usually already know the answer they're waiting for. There's no fixed target, but if you can't remember the last five things you said without most of them being commands, that's worth noticing.

**What's the difference between coaching by instruction and coaching by questioning?**
Instruction tells a player what to do. Questioning, asking something like "who was free?", makes the player work out the answer themselves. Both have a place. Questioning is the one coaches under-use by default — not because it doesn't work, but because it's slower in the moment and harder to do well under pressure.

**Can a coach just say nothing?**
Yes, deliberately. Observing and Silence are both legitimate coaching choices, not gaps in coaching. Observe means gathering information before deciding whether to act. Silence means withholding input so a player has to solve it themselves. Neither costs the player anything to hold in mind, which is part of why they work for young players specifically.
