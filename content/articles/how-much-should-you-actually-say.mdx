*7 min read*

You built the environment right. Space, task, equipment, players — the session is designed so the right decision is the obvious one. So why are you still talking the whole time?

Most coaches don't think of themselves as talkers. But watch the sideline at any U8 session, yours included, and count how often a coach's voice fills a gap that didn't need filling. A pass goes slightly wrong and out comes an instruction, before the player's even had a chance to notice it themselves. A game is flowing fine and it still gets stopped, because there's a coaching point sitting there, unused.

Building the right environment was half the job. This is the other half: what you actually do once the session is running.

## What coaches actually say, when someone's counting

This isn't a guess about coaching instincts. A [season-long study of coaching behavior](https://cdn1.sportngin.com/attachments/document/feaa-2890941/A_season_long_investigation_into_coaching_behaviou.pdf) logged what three collegiate coaches (field hockey, volleyball, basketball) actually said during real sessions, not what they thought they said — over 1,000, 533, and 905 minutes of observation respectively. Instruction made up 27–37% of everything each coach said. Questioning made up only 9–14%, roughly a 3-to-1 gap in instruction's favor. And of the questions that did get asked, closed questions — the kind with one right answer the coach already has in mind — outnumbered open ones by roughly 8 to 1. One coach in the study, asked why his questioning stayed closed, said it plainly: he "wanted the answer immediately, knew what the answer was, and knew that they could give me it."

Worth being straight about: that study is collegiate coaches, not U8, and not soccer. I haven't found the equivalent instrument-and-stopwatch study run on youth soccer coaches specifically. But the pattern it documents — coaches defaulting to Instruct without noticing, and closed questions crowding out open ones — is a coaching-behavior finding, not a sport-specific or age-specific one, and it's worth sitting with regardless. Not because it's a character flaw — stopping to ask an open question and wait for a real answer is genuinely harder than just saying the thing. It's slower in the moment, even if it teaches more over the season. Instruct is the path of least resistance, and coaches default to it the same way water finds the lowest point, not because anyone decided talking more was the better plan.

## Instruct is one tool, not the only one

I use a wider menu with my own coaches, and Instruct is just one entry on it, not the default:

- **Observe.** Gather information before deciding whether to act at all. Says nothing yet.
- **Silence.** Withhold input on purpose, so the player has to solve it themselves.
- **Question.** An open question that makes them think — "Who was free?" — not one with a single right answer you're waiting to hear.
- **Guide and discovery.** A nudge, not the answer — "What did you see over your left shoulder?"
- **Co-create.** Build the solution with the players instead of handing it to them — "What do we want to try in the next three minutes?"
- **Check understanding.** Ask them to tell you back what they took from it.
- **Demonstrate.** Show it instead of describing it.
- **Reframe.** Change how the player sees the moment, not what they did — "That wasn't a bad pass. That was the right idea, a second late."
- **Feedback.** Tell them what happened and what it caused.
- **Reinforce.** Name precisely what was good, so it happens again.
- **Challenge.** Raise the demand on a player who's comfortable.
- **Instruct.** A direct, unambiguous command — "Body between the ball and the defender. Now." Still on the list. Still sometimes exactly right. Just not the only entry.

None of these are paired with a fixed situation. They're a starting point for experimenting with your own group, not a decision tree — the same moment might call for Silence with one player and Instruct with another, depending on what they're ready for.

## Why over-instructing doesn't just slow things down

If you read the first piece in this series, you already know part of why this matters more than it sounds like it should. Children given five explicit instructions on a technique, tracked across 240 practice shots, split in two: kids with higher working-memory capacity kept improving, and kids with lower working-memory capacity got measurably *worse*. Not because the skill was too hard — because holding the instructions in mind while trying to move was the actual obstacle ([Buszard et al., *Frontiers in Psychology*, 2017](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2017.01350/full)).

That's the mechanism underneath this article, not a separate point. Every time Instruct is the reflex instead of a choice, you're not just talking more — you're adding to a working-memory load that's already close to its limit for a meaningful number of your players. Question, Silence, and Guide and discovery aren't softer alternatives to Instruct. They're the ones that don't cost the player anything to hold in mind, because the player is doing the thinking instead of storing your words.

There's a second, separate line of research pointing the same direction, from sport psychology rather than motor learning. A 2024 study followed 325 youth athletes (ages 12–18, across team, individual, and endurance sports) over two points in a season and found that coaching which supported autonomy — giving athletes a real say, asking rather than dictating — predicted higher psychological resilience, which in turn predicted more optimism, which in turn predicted better development outcomes ([Frontiers in Psychology, 2024](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2024.1433171/full)). Worth flagging honestly: that sample skews older than U8, and it's measuring resilience and development rather than a technical skill directly. But it's a second, independent line of evidence — psychological this time, not just cognitive — that asking and involving players does more than just feel nicer. It appears to build something in them that pure instruction doesn't.

## Before any of that: should you be saying anything at all?

Everything above assumes you've already decided to step in. That's not automatic, and it's worth separating the two decisions, because they get solved by different tools.

Whether to intervene at all is a STEP question, not an interaction-style one. If a session is running too easy, too difficult, too slow, or producing the wrong behavior, the first fix is the environment — the space, task, equipment, or players — not your voice. A coach who spots a struggling group and reaches straight for more talking is often fixing a design problem with the wrong tool. Check the environment first. If it's built right and players are still stuck, that's when you're choosing how to say something, not whether to.

So the order is: diagnose with STEP, adjust the environment if that's the actual problem, and only then reach for one of the tools below.

## So when is Instruct actually the right call?

This isn't an argument for never instructing. Some moments genuinely need it: safety, a rule the player doesn't know yet, a skill so new there's nothing for them to discover because they don't have the reference points to discover it with. Demonstrate and Instruct do real work there that Question can't.

The question worth asking isn't "should I ever instruct?" It's "is this one of those moments, or is this just the fastest thing to say?" Most of the time, honestly, it's the second one.

Here's a way to check yourself without overthinking it mid-session: after training, try to remember the last five things you said to players. If four or five of them were you telling someone what to do, that's worth noticing — not as a verdict on you as a coach, but as the same kind of honest data the season-long study collected. You don't need a clipboard and a stopwatch. You just need to actually ask the question, because most of us, like the coaches in that study, think we talk less than we do.

## Where this goes next

Knowing what to say, and when to hold back, is one half of this. The other half is *when* you step in — whether you stop the whole session for a coaching point that could've been a sentence in passing. That's the next piece: how to progress a session without stopping the game every two minutes.

For now: after your next session, try the five-things check. You might talk less than you think. You might not. Either way, you'll actually know.

## Common questions about how much a coach should talk

**How much should a youth soccer coach talk during practice?**
Less than most coaches think. A season-long study of coaching behavior found coaches spend roughly three times as much talk on direct instruction as on questioning, and most of that questioning is closed rather than open — meaning coaches usually already know the answer they're waiting for. There's no fixed target, but if you can't remember the last five things you said without most of them being commands, that's worth noticing.

**What's the difference between coaching by instruction and coaching by questioning?**
Instruction tells a player what to do. Questioning — asking something like "who was free?" — makes the player work out the answer themselves. Both have a place, but questioning is the one coaches under-use by default, not because it doesn't work but because it's slower in the moment and harder to do well under pressure.

**Should a coach ever just say nothing?**
Yes — deliberately. Observing (gathering information before deciding whether to act) and Silence (withholding input so a player has to solve it themselves) are both legitimate coaching choices, not gaps in coaching. They cost the player nothing to hold in mind, unlike a stream of instructions, which is part of why they work for young players specifically.
