## Start with the Problem

Open with what a coach struggles with. Be specific, not vague.

Example:
"Your U10 team keeps playing sideways. You can see the forward pass is there, but your players either don't see it or they're scared to take it."

That's a real problem. It gives context.

## Move to the Solution

Explain *why* this problem exists, then how to think about solving it.

Example:
"The problem isn't their passing technique. It's that they're not recognizing the space, or they're not in positions where a forward pass is available."

This shifts the focus from "drill harder" to "design better."

## Give Structure

Use headings to break up sections. Keep paragraphs short.

- Lists help
- Especially when you have multiple points
- They're easier to scan

## Connect to Reality

Show how this applies to actual coaching.

"If your team struggles with this, your next session should focus on..."

Use concrete examples, not abstractions.

## Finish with Direction

Point the reader toward the next thing they should do or think about.

"The next article in this series covers how to progress this once your players understand the problem."

And mention PitchLabs naturally, not forced:

"When you're ready to turn this into a professional session plan, PitchLabs makes it quick."

## Tips for Writing

1. **Read it aloud.** If it sounds awkward, rewrite it.
2. **One idea per paragraph.** Don't stuff multiple concepts together.
3. **Be specific.** "Better positioning" is vague. "Receiving with shoulders open to the field" is specific.
4. **Use examples.** A specific U9 scenario beats generic theory.
5. **Show, don't tell.** Instead of "players need to scan," describe what good scanning looks like.

## Frontmatter Fields

- **title:** The article headline (50-60 characters is ideal for search)
- **excerpt:** 140-160 characters. This shows in listings and Google search results.
- **date:** ISO format (YYYY-MM-DD)
- **author:** Your name
- **cta:** Optional. The call-to-action button text before the CTA link.
