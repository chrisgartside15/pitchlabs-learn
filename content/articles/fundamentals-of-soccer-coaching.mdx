Watch enough youth soccer training and you'll notice something: the coaches running the best-organized sessions aren't always developing the best decision-makers. Cones out on time, a clean progression from warm-up to scrimmage, every activity planned to the minute — and still, a team that freezes the moment the pattern breaks. A well-run activity and good coaching are not the same thing. Mixing them up is the most common mistake I see, and I've made it myself plenty of times.

Think about your last session before you read any further. How much of it was a player repeating something you already knew the outcome of, and how much of it was a player actually facing a moment where more than one thing could reasonably happen?

This page is the broad version of an idea the rest of this site keeps working through from different angles: [how you build the environment](/articles/why-your-u8-practice-feels-like-chaos), [what you say once you're in it](/articles/how-much-should-you-actually-say), [how you physically step in to say it](/articles/progress-a-session-without-stopping-the-game), and, over time, more pieces than that. Each gets its own article. This one is about the shape of the idea underneath all of them.

## What separates good coaching from a good activity

An activity can be well-designed and still not be coaching much of anything. A passing drill with sharp technique, good tempo, and full engagement can still ask nothing of a player except to repeat a movement. It's rehearsal. Useful, sometimes necessary, but it isn't the same job as developing a player who can read a game and act on it.

Researchers who reviewed youth soccer coaching found sessions split roughly two-thirds isolated drilling, one-third small-sided or conditioned games, and that ratio barely moved even at nine years old ([Ford et al., cited via Cushion, Ford & Williams, *Journal of Sports Sciences*, 2012](https://doi.org/10.1080/02640414.2012.721930)). That's not a criticism of the coaches in that data. It's what happens by default. Drilling is easier to plan, easier to control, and easier to feel good about at the end of a session, because you watched forty clean touches happen in a row. A conditioned game is messier to run and harder to guarantee will "work." Which one was your last session, honestly?

## Perceive, decide, execute

Every action on a field, however small, runs through the same sequence: a player takes in information about what's around them, decides what to do with it, then executes the movement. Coaches sometimes call the first part scanning. Sport scientists call the whole thing information processing. Either way, it's three separate skills.

Most coaching attention, including most of what's written on this site so far, goes to the last two. [STEP](/articles/why-your-u8-practice-feels-like-chaos) is the tool I use for shaping what a player has to decide — space, task, equipment, player numbers, built so the decision you want shows up naturally instead of getting narrated from the sideline. [The Coaching Intervention Wheel](/articles/how-much-should-you-actually-say) is about what happens around the execution — how you interact without quietly making the decision for the player yourself.

That leaves perceiving. What a player actually sees, and how early they see it, before either the decision or the execution even starts. It gets the least attention of the three in most training, including a fair amount of my own, and it's usually the actual bottleneck — a player who scans early has more time to decide well and more time to execute cleanly, and a player who doesn't is playing catch-up on both no matter how good the environment or the coaching around them is. That's a harder problem than the other two, and it doesn't have a tidy answer here yet.

## Why a coach's job changes by age group

None of this looks the same at six as it does at sixteen. A player's working memory, their ability to hold and use several pieces of information at once, develops with age. Five explicit coaching points before a drill was enough to measurably hurt performance in children with lower working-memory capacity in one study of eight-to-ten-year-olds ([Buszard et al., *Frontiers in Psychology*, 2017](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2017.01350/full)). That's not an argument for saying nothing. It's a reason the same session, run the same way, asks a very different amount of a seven-year-old than it does of a fourteen-year-old.

Good age-appropriate coaching models handle this by deliberately limiting what younger players are asked to decide, not by pretending the whole game is available to them from day one. A lot of coach education for the youngest age groups leaves out entire tactical layers on purpose, not because young players can't eventually understand them, but because trying to teach them too early adds decisions nobody at that age is ready to make well. The job isn't to withhold the game. It's to build the size of decision a player can actually handle, and let that grow as they do.

Would you recognize it if a session was asking too much of a six-year-old? Not too much running, or too much standing around — too much *thinking*, packed into a picture they're not ready to read yet. Most coaches are good at spotting the first two. Fewer are looking for the third.

## What this means for a session

Before you plan the next one, ask what a player is actually being asked to do in each activity: repeat a movement, decide something, or perceive something in the first place. Most sessions lean hard on the first two and barely touch the third. That's the gap most worth closing, and it's the one this page doesn't fully resolve — the specific pieces of it get worked out as they're written, one at a time, the same way the environment and interaction pieces already have been.

## Common questions about coaching fundamentals

**What's the difference between coaching and running drills?**
A drill trains a movement in isolation, usually without a real decision attached. Coaching, in the fuller sense, develops a player's ability to perceive a situation, decide what to do about it, and then execute — which only happens when the activity actually contains a decision worth making, not just a technique worth repeating.

**Do young players need to learn decision-making, or should they master technique first?**
Both, at the same time, in different proportions. Technique needs isolated repetition to build reliably, especially early on. But decision-making only develops by making real decisions under some pressure, so a session that's entirely technical drilling is leaving out half the job, even at the youngest ages.

**Why do coaching methods need to change so much by age group?**
Because the amount of information a player can hold and use at once changes with age. A method that works for a fourteen-year-old can genuinely hurt an eight-year-old's performance if it asks for more than they can process while moving — not because younger players are less capable, but because what they can process is different.
