I haven't found a single study that tests whether stopping the game for a huddle teaches young players less than a quick word on the move. What the research does show is that coaches instruct a lot, more than once a minute across studies of youth soccer, and misjudge how much ([Cushion, Ford & Williams, *Journal of Sports Sciences*, 2012](https://doi.org/10.1080/02640414.2012.721930)). Not all of that involves stopping play, since plenty of it is said while the game runs.

So most of this piece is practical judgment, not findings, and I'll say where. The case for it rests on something you can see without a study: when a whole session stops for a message meant for one player, fifteen players stand and wait for something that wasn't for them.

Say the environment's built right, and you've thought about what to say once you step in and when to stay quiet and let a player work it out. There's a third decision underneath both of those, and it's easy never to separate it out: how do you interrupt play to say anything at all? The cost isn't the same every time.

## The whole wheel, not just one ring

<DefinitionBox term="The Coaching Intervention Wheel">
A framework for the other half of environment — not what you build, but how and when you step into it. Three layers: who you're coaching, how you interact once you've decided to say something, and how you stop the game to say it.
</DefinitionBox>

Like STEP, this isn't mine. Each layer of it (who you're talking to, how you say it, how you stop play) shows up in plenty of coach education; this is the version I've assembled and use. The last two pieces in this series have already been using it without naming the whole thing.

**At the center: who you're coaching.** Every intervention is aimed at one of four sizes of audience:

- **Individual** — one player.
- **Group** — players across units who share an area of the field, like a left back and the left winger in front of them.
- **Unit** — a full line of the team: defensive, midfield, or attacking.
- **Team** — everyone.

**The inner ring: how you interact, once you've stepped in.** Twelve ways to say something, from saying nothing at all to a direct command — Observe, Silence, Question and the rest of the menu from [the last piece in this series](/articles/how-much-should-you-actually-say), which covers why Instruct doesn't have to be the default.

**The outer ring: how you physically stop the game to say it.** Neither of the first two pieces got to this layer. It's what the rest of this piece is about.

## Six ways to stop a session, and what each one costs

Every way of stepping into a live session is one of six moves, and each has its own price tag in playing time. The times are my own rough estimates from running these, not measured figures:

- **In-flow.** One sentence, to one player, while everything else keeps moving. **Free.**
- **Drinks break.** Coach inside a pause you were already taking. **Free.**
- **Pull aside.** Take one or two players out while the rest keep playing. 20–30 seconds.
- **Freeze frame.** Everything stops, players hold their positions exactly where they are. 30–45 seconds.
- **Huddle.** Bring the whole group in, balls down. 60–90 seconds.
- **Walkthrough.** Rehearse the movement at walking pace, no pressure. 60–120 seconds.

Two of those six cost you no playing time. Four of them spend playing time you don't get back. Naming them helps stop a huddle being the automatic move.

## Time isn't the only cost

Each of these six also has a natural audience — which of the four groups above it reaches — and to me, matching the stoppage to that audience matters as much as watching the clock. An in-flow word fits an individual. A pull-aside fits a group or a unit. A huddle or a freeze frame reaches the whole team.

A mistake I see a lot is picking the wrong *size* of stoppage, more than picking an expensive one. A coach spots one player drifting out of position, and instead of a five-second word on the move, the whole session stops for a huddle. That's the fifteen-players-waiting picture from the top of this piece, and it costs attention as well as playing time: the same kind of noise [the first piece in this series](/articles/why-your-practice-feels-like-chaos) covers in more depth. So I try to match the stoppage to who needs to hear it.

## The two free moves

**In-flow** costs no playing time, which makes it the cheapest tool you have and also the easiest to overuse. A single sentence to a single player, delivered without stopping anything, pairs well with a quick Instruct, a Challenge, or a Reinforce. It's still one more thing for that player to hold in mind while they play, so the one-idea discipline from [the last piece](/articles/how-much-should-you-actually-say) applies to in-flow words too.

**Drinks break** is free in the same sense, but with a catch: free in playing time isn't the same as free in attention. Players are resting, and often switched off in a way they aren't mid-rep. Use the moment, but keep it to one idea. Stack three coaching points into a thirty-second water break and you're overloading young players the same way you would during live play, just standing still while you do it.

## When a bigger stoppage earns its cost

The other four aren't wrong to use. They're just not free, so I'd want each one to be doing something a cheaper option couldn't.

**Pull aside** earns its 20–30 seconds when the problem belongs to a couple of players specifically and the rest of the group doesn't need to hear it. It's the scaled-up version of an in-flow word, still targeted, just needing a few more seconds to land.

**Freeze frame** is worth its 30–45 seconds when the exact picture, at this exact instant, is the whole teaching point — a shape of play, a spacing problem, a moment you want every player to see rather than have described to them after the fact. Used well, it's showing instead of telling, and it can do something ten separate in-flow words can't: get a whole team seeing the same picture at once. That's the fair case against everything else in this piece, and it's why freeze frames aren't something to avoid, only something to choose on purpose.

Both are worth using deliberately rather than reflexively. One long-standing idea in motor learning is that feedback delivered the instant a mistake happens can crowd out what the player would otherwise have noticed themselves, and lead to fixing something that didn't need fixing (Wulf & Shea, in *Skill Acquisition in Sport*, 2004, [cited via Cushion et al., 2012](https://doi.org/10.1080/02640414.2012.721930)). The evidence for it is more mixed than it sounds, though. A 2022 meta-analysis pooling the studies that tested whether giving feedback less often improves learning found no reliable effect either way, and described most of those studies as badly underpowered ([McKay et al., *Psychology of Sport and Exercise*, 2022](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.psychsport.2022.102165)). Treat "less feedback means more learning" as a possibility, not a rule. The case for pausing is simpler: a beat of silence before you freeze the picture or pull a player aside gives them a chance to spot it first, and costs you nothing.

**Huddle** earns its 60–90 seconds when the message genuinely belongs to everyone: resetting the objective at the start of a phase, or regrouping after a run of chaos that needs a shared reset, not five individual corrections.

**Walkthrough** earns its 60–120 seconds when a movement needs to be seen at walking pace once, without the pressure of live play, before it'll show up correctly when the game speeds back up. It's also the natural home for the case [part one](/articles/why-your-practice-feels-like-chaos) covers, where players can't yet do the thing the game is asking for and need it taught before the game can teach them when to use it.

These pairings aren't rules. Try one, watch what happens with your group, and adjust.

## Three moments in the same game

Back to the Switch of Play game from [Why Your Soccer Practice Feels Like Chaos](/articles/why-your-practice-feels-like-chaos), where a goal counts double if it's scored on the opposite side to where your team won the ball. Different moments in it call for different ways of stepping in.

- **One player keeps driving at the near goal.** The message is for one player, so it can go in-flow: a single nudge as play carries on, like "Who was free?", and nobody else has to stop.
- **Nobody on either team is switching.** After a few minutes of that, the message belongs to everyone, and it's about a picture. That's where a freeze frame earns its cost: stop play the moment a team wins the ball, and let every player see where the space is.
- **They see the switch but can't get the ball across.** The decision isn't the problem, so stopping to talk about decisions won't fix it. A short walkthrough, or a few minutes of focused passing over that distance, then back into the game.

That's how I'd match them, not the only options. A huddle to reset the game's objective could do the second job too. It just costs more of everyone's time.

## Where this leaves you

STEP handles whether the environment itself needs to change. The inner ring of the Coaching Intervention Wheel handles what you say once you've decided to step in. This piece, the outer ring, handles how you physically interrupt to say it, and what that interruption costs. In the way I use them, all three are one idea — see [The Fundamentals of Soccer Coaching](/articles/fundamentals-of-soccer-coaching) for the version without the tools attached.

Next session, keep count of your huddles, or [film ten minutes](/articles/see-your-own-coaching) and count them afterwards. If most of what you said could have been an in-flow word, that's where to start.

## Common questions about stopping a youth soccer session to coach

**How often should I stop a youth soccer practice to coach?**
There's no research-backed number. Two of the six ways to interrupt play, in-flow and drinks break, cost no playing time at all, and it's easy to reach for a whole-group huddle instead, which is the most expensive option on the list. One check I find useful before stopping play: would a free option do the same job?

**Do coaches really instruct more than they realize?**
Studies that observed real youth soccer sessions found coaches instructing more than once a minute on average, and found that coaches' own ratings of their behavior match what was observed less well than their players' ratings do ([Cushion, Ford & Williams, 2012](https://doi.org/10.1080/02640414.2012.721930)). It's not a discipline problem. It's just hard to track accurately from the inside of your own session.

**What's the difference between a freeze frame and a huddle?**
A freeze frame holds players exactly where they are on the field, so the specific spatial picture is the teaching point. A huddle brings everyone in with balls down, better suited to a message that isn't tied to a specific spot on the field. Freeze frame costs less time, 30–45 seconds against 60–90, but both stop the whole session, so neither is free.

**Is it bad to use a lot of drinks breaks to coach?**
Not in terms of playing time, since they're free. But players are resting and often switched off in a way they aren't during live play, so a drinks break is the wrong moment to stack multiple coaching points. Keep it to one idea, the same discipline that matters during live play too.
