If your U8 soccer practice feels chaotic, the problem usually isn't your coaching. It's the environment you built.

The same eight-year-olds who can't sit still for five minutes in a classroom will chase a ball for an hour without anyone telling them to, then fall apart the moment you try to organize what they're doing. That has nothing to do with attention. It's about what you actually built for them to learn from.

I've been coaching for over 15 years, and I still catch myself doing it: asking a player for one or two things more than the session needs, more than they can hold in their head at that age. It's an easy habit even when you know better, because in the moment, saying one more thing always feels like coaching.

## What if the chaos isn't a coaching problem?

There are two ways to get a player to make a good decision. You can tell them what to do — more instructions, more stopping the game, more of your voice competing for their attention. Or you can build a practice where the right decision is the obvious one, because of how you've set up the space, the task, the equipment and the players.

The second way asks more of you before training and less of you during it. It also asks less of the player, which at this age matters more than most of us like to admit. Children learn a lot from what an environment lets them discover, and not much from instructions they're still trying to hold in their heads while they move.

I didn't come up with this on a car journey home. It lines up with a researched approach to practice design called the constraints-led approach, built on decades of ecological dynamics work — players learn by interacting with their environment, not by being told what to do — from sport scientists like Keith Davids and colleagues. [The FA teaches a version of it](https://www.thefa.com/bootroom/resources/coaching/how-to-use-constraints-in-your-coaching-session) through Ben Bartlett's "restrict, reward, relate" framing. Different name, same idea: design the game rather than narrate it.

## The STEP framework

<DefinitionBox term="STEP">
STEP is a coaching framework for adjusting a session by changing four things: Space, Task, Equipment, Players.
</DefinitionBox>

Make the area bigger or smaller. Change the rules or how players score. Swap the goals or balls. Change the number of players on each team. [The FA teaches a version of this](https://learn.englandfootball.com/articles/resources/2022/make-coaching-personal-with-the-step-framework) today, so it isn't mine and it isn't new. I keep coming back to it because it turns "this session isn't working" into four specific questions instead of one vague worry.

- **Space.** Can I change the area to create more time, pressure, or movement? Squeeze a 4v4 into 25x20 and you get faster decisions and more pressure on the first touch. Stretch it to 40x30 and you reward the player brave enough to take their first touch forward instead of sideways.
- **Task.** Can I simplify it, add one condition, or change how players score? Not four conditions stacked on each other — one constraint that pushes the specific decision you want to see. Make a goal worth double when it's scored on the opposite side to where you won the ball, and the team in possession has to scan for space and a wide option before they commit, instead of just driving at the nearest goal. An old mentor of mine put it simply: chase five rabbits and you catch none. Chase one and you've at least got a chance.
- **Equipment.** Can I use goals, gates, zones, or different balls to shape the behavior, instead of shaping it with my voice? Small goals at both ends instead of one big one give a switch-of-play rule somewhere to go. The reward for scanning has to be real, not just a coaching point shouted from the sideline.
- **Players.** Can I adjust numbers, add a neutral, or create an overload? Overloads buy space and time for the team in numbers-up. Underloads force quicker decisions out of the team defending.

Get these four right and you barely need to coach with your voice. Get them wrong and you spend the whole session narrating over a practice that's fighting you.

## When a session isn't working

Start with what's actually going wrong.

**Too easy?** Players are succeeding without effort. Try Players before you shrink the game for everyone — often it's only part of the group finding it easy.

**Too difficult?** Constant breakdowns switch young players off fast. Simplify the Task first, then rebuild the challenge gradually.

**Too slow?** That's rarely lazy players. Usually it's too much standing around, and Players is the fastest fix: smaller teams, more games running, nobody waiting for a turn.

**Right game, wrong behavior?** If players aren't doing the thing you designed the session for, ask whether the game actually *requires* that behavior to win. If they can succeed without it, they will — that's game intelligence, not defiance. Reward the behavior you want in the scoring rather than banning the workaround.

Two of those four have decent research behind them. The too-easy and too-difficult ends are what motor learning researchers call the challenge point: learning stalls at either extreme and peaks somewhere in the middle ([Guadagnoli & Lee, *Journal of Motor Behavior*, 2004](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/abs/10.3200/JMBR.36.2.212-224)). The too-slow fix has support too — a small study of U14 players found significantly more ball contacts, successful passes and overall involvement in 3v3 than in 5v5 ([Alcântara et al., *Sport Sciences for Health*, 2021](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11332-020-00715-6)). Fewer players per team means more of the ball for each of them, and that logic holds harder at U8, where standing in a line kills a session faster than it does at fourteen.

## Why young players can't hold five instructions

The reason a seven-year-old can chase a ball for an hour but can't sit through five minutes of instruction has nothing to do with attitude. It's capacity.

Researchers took 90 children aged eight to ten, measured their working memory, and had them practice basketball shooting across 240 shots. Before every block, the children got five explicit instructions about technique. The kids with higher working-memory capacity improved steadily, roughly what you'd expect. The kids with lower working-memory capacity got *worse* ([Buszard et al., *Frontiers in Psychology*, 2017](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2017.01350/full)). Not because the skill was beyond them — holding five instructions in mind while trying to move was the actual obstacle.

Five instructions. Not fifteen. That was enough to make a group of children measurably worse at a skill they were practicing 240 times.

How many coaching points are you stacking on top of a busy environment right now? A session doing that isn't teaching four things. For a good number of your players it's teaching none of them, while looking busy. Remember the rabbits.

A packed, multi-part session *looks* like good coaching. Lots happening, lots planned, a plan you could hand your DOC without embarrassment. But a well-built environment does more teaching with less noise than a coach narrating ever could. Here's the test I'd use instead: could a player on your team tell you what they were working on after today's session? If not, too much was competing for their attention, however well it read on paper.

## What to cut from your session plan

Look at your session and ask which pieces are shaping the environment on purpose, and which are there because they make you feel prepared. Usually it's:

- The fourth activity, when three would have done the job properly
- Two or three conditions running at once, none of them clearly doing work on its own
- The backup plan you wrote in case the first one didn't land, which is really a plan to abandon the environment you designed the moment it gets noisy

Cutting these isn't lowering your standards. You're choosing to run one environment well instead of three half-attentively.

Watching 17 coaches across 22 teams, two or three sessions a week outside my own, this is the pattern I see most: not bad coaches, but sessions carrying one activity more than they need.

## Common questions about U8 soccer practice

**Why is my U8 soccer practice so chaotic?**
Usually because the environment is doing the opposite of what you're asking for. If the space, the numbers and the scoring don't require the behavior you want, no amount of instruction from the sideline will produce it. Change the game before you change your coaching.

**How many coaching points should you give a U8 player?**
There's no magic number, but the research is a useful gut check: five explicit instructions was enough to measurably hurt performance in children with lower working memory capacity. If you're stacking more than one or two coaching points into a single activity, you're probably past the point of diminishing returns for a lot of your players.

**How many players should be on each team in a U8 practice?**
Smaller than you think. 3v3 and 4v4 give every player far more touches than 5v5 or bigger, and less time standing in line. If a session feels slow, cutting team sizes and running more games at once is usually a faster fix than anything you say.
