A lot of session plans describe what happens when everything goes right. Activity, setup, coaching points, time. The part that decides whether a session works is what you'll change when it doesn't, and that's the part plans most often leave out.

This is the pillar guide for session design on this site. It walks through planning one youth session, step by step, and links out to a fuller article at each step. It's one way to plan, the way I do it, not the only way. None of it is a new method: each step borrows from something established, and the research behind each one is linked where it exists. Take the parts that fit your players and change the rest.

## Step 1: Pick one thing the session is about

Before any activities, decide what one idea the session is for. Not four. An old mentor of mine put it simply: chase five rabbits and you catch none.

There's research behind the instinct. In one study of eight-to-ten-year-olds, five instructions before each block of practice helped the children with more working memory and made the children with less of it worse ([Buszard et al., *Frontiers in Psychology*, 2017](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2017.01350/full)). A session about one thing is more likely to land for more of the group. [Why Your Soccer Practice Feels Like Chaos](/articles/why-your-practice-feels-like-chaos) covers this in more depth.

## Step 2: Choose a shape

U.S. Soccer's grassroots courses use a structure called Play-Practice-Play: players start with a game, move into an activity or two focused on the session's idea, then finish with a game where they use it ([U.S. Soccer, "Five Things to Know About Play-Practice-Play"](https://www.ussoccer.com/stories/2018/02/five-things-to-know-about-playpracticeplay)). It's a sensible default for young players, because it keeps most of the session game-like and still leaves room for focused work. It isn't the only shape that works. Some coaches use whole-part-whole, some run a session that's games from start to finish, and the right one depends on your players and what the session is about.

That fits the comparison research reasonably well. In the studies that pitted game-based practice against drill-based practice, drills didn't come out ahead even on technique, and games did better on tactical skills ([Bromilow et al., *Sports Medicine – Open*, 2025](https://doi.org/10.1186/s40798-025-00893-y)). Only a few of those studies involved young children, so it's a lean, not a verdict.

The middle "practice" block is where direct teaching belongs when players can't yet do the thing the games are asking for. [Teach It or Let the Game Teach It?](/articles/teach-it-or-let-the-game-teach-it) covers how to tell whether a group needs it.

## Step 3: Build each activity with STEP

For each activity, set the four things that shape what players do:

- **Space:** how big the area is, which controls time and pressure.
- **Task:** the rules and how players score, which is where you reward the behavior you want.
- **Equipment:** goals, gates, zones, balls.
- **Players:** team sizes, neutrals, overloads.

STEP isn't mine. [The FA teaches a version of it](https://learn.englandfootball.com/articles/resources/2022/make-coaching-personal-with-the-step-framework), and it lines up with the constraints-led approach to practice design. [Why Your Soccer Practice Feels Like Chaos](/articles/why-your-practice-feels-like-chaos) covers it in full, with an interactive tool.

For team sizes specifically, smaller usually means more touches, and the players who gain most are the least involved ones. [Small-Sided Games for Young Players](/articles/small-sided-games) has the research, and a tool for splitting however many players turned up into small games.

## Step 4: Decide now what you'll change if it goes wrong

When an activity isn't working mid-session, the quickest fix to reach for is usually talking more. A change you've already thought through is usually a better one, and it's much easier to make in the moment if it's written down.

Activities tend to go wrong in one of four ways. For each activity, write one line per problem: what you'd see, and the one thing you'd change. Here's what that looks like for the 3v3 switch-of-play game from [Why Your Soccer Practice Feels Like Chaos](/articles/why-your-practice-feels-like-chaos), where a goal counts double if it's scored on the opposite side to where the team won the ball:

The changes are examples for that one game, not rules. What carries over is the pattern: when it's too easy or too slow, try adjusting Players; when it's too difficult, simplify the Task; when players aren't doing what you designed it for, change the scoring rather than banning what they're doing instead.

That four-way split is how I use STEP, not a research finding. The too-easy and too-difficult ends do line up with the challenge point framework, a well-used theory in motor learning which proposes that learning stalls when practice is too easy or too hard for the learner ([Guadagnoli & Lee, *Journal of Motor Behavior*, 2004](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/abs/10.3200/JMBR.36.2.212-224)). The interactive tool in [Why Your Soccer Practice Feels Like Chaos](/articles/why-your-practice-feels-like-chaos) walks through all four.

## Step 5: Plan how you'll step in

It helps to decide ahead of time what you'll watch for and how you'll interrupt when you need to. Two questions cover most of it:

- **What will you say, and how?** Instruct is one option among many: questions, deliberate silence, demonstrating, reinforcing what went well. [How Much Should You Actually Say?](/articles/how-much-should-you-actually-say) covers the full menu.
- **How will you stop play, if you need to?** A word to one player on the move costs no playing time. A whole-group huddle costs a minute or more of everyone's time. [How to Coach Without Stopping the Game](/articles/coach-without-stopping-the-game) covers the options and when each earns its cost.

## Step 6: Fit it to the age

The same plan asks very different things of a six-year-old and a ten-year-old. Working memory keeps growing through childhood, formats change, and younger players are more often beginners at the basic skills. [Coaching U6–U10](/articles/coaching-u6-to-u10) covers what changes and what matters most at those ages.

## Step 7: Check afterwards

Two quick checks after training:

- **Could a player tell you what they worked on?** Ask a couple. If they can't, too much was competing for their attention.
- **What were the last five things you said?** If most were instructions, that's worth noticing. [How to See Your Own Coaching](/articles/see-your-own-coaching) covers better ways to find out.

## Where the evidence is thin

The steps above borrow from research where it exists, but a session plan is mostly practical judgment. The research that compares game-based and drill-based practice is nine studies, few with young children. A 2023 systematic review of the soccer research on feedback, demonstration, focus of attention and practice schedules found most of the findings weren't unanimous ([Pacheco et al., *International Journal of Sports Science & Coaching*, 2023](https://doi.org/10.1177/17479541231168930)). Play-Practice-Play is a governing body's recommended structure, not a tested one. Use the template as a starting point and change it once you've seen how your own group responds.

## Common questions about planning a soccer session

**How do you plan a youth soccer training session?**
One way: pick one idea the session is about, choose a shape (a game, a focused practice, a game is a good default), build each activity by setting the space, task, equipment and players, write down what you'll change if it goes wrong, plan how you'll step in, and check afterwards whether players can tell you what they worked on.

**How long should a youth soccer session be?**
It depends on age and your club's schedule. What matters more than the length is how much of it players spend playing rather than waiting, which smaller teams and fewer activities both help with.

**How many activities should a session have?**
Usually fewer than you'd think. In my experience, three activities run well beat four run in a rush, and a Play-Practice-Play structure needs only three.

**What is Play-Practice-Play?**
A session structure used in U.S. Soccer's grassroots coaching courses: players start with a game, move into one or two focused activities, then finish with a game where they use what they practiced.
