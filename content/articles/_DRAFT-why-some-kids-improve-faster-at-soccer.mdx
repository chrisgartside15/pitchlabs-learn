<TLDR>
- In the one study that compared what elite English academy players did between 6 and 12, the players who went on to turn professional had logged more informal soccer play. They hadn't done more organised practice or played more matches.
- Two or three club sessions a week is a normal baseline, and the ball is shared between everyone in a session. The players who play more outside sessions get a bigger share of it.
- "More" doesn't have to mean paid lessons. The one randomised trial found of extra coaching for 9 to 12-year-olds saw no more improvement than a control group on its test.
- Quality counts: how much they play, whether they enjoy it, and whether the week is overscheduled.
</TLDR>

Some kids seem to get better every month, while others in the same team and the same sessions stay roughly where they are. A lot of families train two or three times a week with their club and do nothing else, then wonder why their player isn't improving as quickly as the kids who play more.

## What separated the players who progressed

[Ford, Ward, Hodges and Williams (2009)](https://msl.kin.educ.ubc.ca/node/117) looked back at the early histories of elite English academy players, comparing those who went on to professional contracts at 16 with those who didn't. Between ages 6 and 12, the players who progressed had logged more hours of soccer play each year. They hadn't logged more organised practice, more competition or more time in other sports.

Both academy groups did more organised practice than recreational players, so practice separated the academy players from recreational ones, and play separated those who progressed from those who didn't. It's a retrospective study of recalled activity in English academies, so it shows an association and can't prove play caused the progress.

Play here means informal soccer: kick-abouts, street games, the park. It isn't extra sessions with a coach.

## What a club schedule gives a player

Even a well-run session shares the ball between everyone in it, and more players means a smaller share each. In studies of ten-year-olds, moving from 7v7 to 4v4 doubled the number of actions per player per minute ([Small-Sided Games for Young Players](/articles/small-sided-games)). So the format a child plays in matters, and so does how much they play outside it.

## What more can look like

- A wall and a ball.
- A kick-about in the park or a street game with whoever's around.
- Small games, 2v2 or 3v3, with friends.
- A local small-sided or futsal league, where the ball is in play and the numbers are small.

None of it needs a coach, and none of it needs to be organised. It needs a ball and some space.

## What private coaching adds

A strong coach can give a child what a team session can't: attention to what that child needs, and an experience that makes them want to keep playing. What the research can say about it is thinner than the marketing.

The one randomised trial found, [Sørensen, Dalen and Lagestad (2024)](https://doi.org/10.3390/sports12120345), gave 9 to 12-year-olds eleven weekly 75-minute sessions of 1v1 and small-sided games and compared them with a control group on a timed skills course. Both groups improved, and the difference between them wasn't significant. The authors note the limits: eleven sessions, a test that didn't measure decision-making, and a small sample. It doesn't show private coaching doesn't work. It shows the case for it is unproven.

A coach who is technically sharp and makes a child dread going is unlikely to help.

## Cost and overload

Average family spending on a child's main sport reached $1,016 in 2024, up 46% since 2019, with private instruction one of the drivers ([Aspen Institute Project Play, 2025](https://projectplay.org/news/2025/2/24/project-play-survey-family-spending-on-youth-sports-rises-46-over-five-years)). And sports medicine consensus statements advise against overscheduling and early specialisation, because of the risk of overuse injury and burnout ([AOSSM, 2016](https://scholarworks.indianapolis.iu.edu/items/55d6bc87-42d6-44b0-b0d0-2999bb653809)).

More isn't always better. A kick-about costs nothing and a rest day is part of the week.

## A way to see your own child's week

At your child's next session, watch for ten minutes and count how many times they touch the ball. Then count the same ten minutes in a kick-about with friends. The difference is usually the point.

## Common questions

**Why isn't my child improving at soccer?**
Two things are worth knowing. The one study that tracked what separated players who progressed pointed to the amount of informal play between 6 and 12. And birth date matters: studies of US elite youth pathways find players born early in the selection year over-represented, so a child who is young for the year can look behind for reasons that have little to do with ability.

**Is private soccer training worth it?**
It can be, if it's small-group, game-like and enjoyable, the coach is good with children, and the cost doesn't crowd out free play or rest. The one randomised trial found no extra gain on its test, so the evidence doesn't say it's necessary.

**How many times a week should a kid practise soccer?**
I haven't found a research-based number for extra practice. Sports medicine statements advise against overscheduling and recommend rest, and a sensible check is whether your child still wants to play.

**Does a younger player fall behind if they only train with their club?**
Not necessarily. Club sessions are the baseline, and the difference in the research was in informal play. It's about the total amount a child plays, not about which sessions they're in.
