<TLDR>
- A parent shouting "Go!" at a young player with the ball sounds supportive, but a young player may hear it as an instruction and go, even when the coach wants something else. That's my experience with U8 to U12 players, not a study finding.
- In one interview study, 29 of 36 young female athletes said parents shouldn't coach from the sideline, because it was confusing and could contradict their coach.
- I couldn't find a study showing that sideline instruction lowers performance in a game, or one comparing age groups.
- Parents mostly want to help. Explaining why a shout can confuse, and giving them something to say instead, usually works better than asking them to be quiet.
</TLDR>

This is written for coaches of U8 to U12 players, the range I coach. Older age groups are a different job, and I'd rather have a coach who works with them write that piece.

A parent shouts "Go, Mia!" and Mia, who has the ball at her feet, goes.

It sounds like the most harmless thing a parent can say. But a young player hears "go" from the person she most wants to please while she has the ball, and the chances are she'll do exactly that, whether or not it's the right idea. She might have been about to pass, or look up, or play it back to a teammate. What I see with younger players is that they hear the word as an instruction, because for them it nearly always is one.

## What a young player is dealing with in that moment

At U8 to U12, a player on the ball is taking in a lot. They're trying to remember what they practiced, show what they've learned, and choose between several options in a second or two, all while a coach, a teammate and a parent are each saying something different. Adding a voice they trust to that is not neutral.

I see it most with the younger players in that range. There's no study I've found that compares ages, so that's my observation. What research there is points in the same direction: in an interview study led by Camilla Knight, 36 girls aged about 13 were asked how they wanted their parents to behave at games. Twenty-nine said parents shouldn't try to coach the team, and they described it as confusing, particularly when a parent's call contradicted what their coach had said ([Knight, Neely & Holt, *Journal of Applied Sport Psychology*, 2011](https://doi.org/10.1080/10413200.2010.525589)). That's interviews with girls around 13, recruited at summer camps in Canada, and the authors say it shouldn't be stretched far beyond that group. If anything, it suggests the problem doesn't go away with age.

There's also the question of how much a child can use at once. In a study of 90 children aged eight to ten learning basketball shooting, children with lower working memory got worse when they were given several explicit instructions before each block ([Buszard et al., *Frontiers in Psychology*, 2017](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2017.01350/full)). That was coaching instruction on a shooting task, not a parent shouting in a game, so it doesn't show that sideline noise hurts. It does suggest why one more voice might be hard to use.

I couldn't find a study that tests whether instructions shouted during a game change how young players perform. Two small surveys point at pressure more than at instruction. In 80 Italian male soccer players, they reported more parental involvement and pressure than they wanted, and less praise and understanding ([Bonavolontà et al., 2021](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34444446/)). In 67 Australian youth athletes, more negative parent behavior on the sideline went with more antisocial behavior from the player ([McCabe, Tsiros & Crozier, 2024](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38239890/)). Both rely on what the players reported, and neither shows cause.

## Start from what the parent is trying to do

Almost nobody shouts "go" to cause a problem. They want their child to do well, they want them to feel they're supported, and they're excited. It helps to say that out loud. A parent who feels told off will often stop listening, and one who hears "I know you're trying to help" usually leans in.

What parents often don't see is how the game looks from the pitch. A child who has just received the ball isn't blank, waiting for a prompt. They're already deciding, with a plan that came from the coach and the practice before. A shout can cut across that plan.

One thing that might make it concrete: ask them to picture someone calling out directions while they try to do something they're still learning, like parallel parking or typing quickly. Help can feel like noise when you're mid-task and still thinking it through. {/* REVIEW: Chris to keep or replace the analogy. */}

## Give them something to say instead

Telling parents to be quiet leaves a gap, and most parents want to join in. A few things I'd suggest they say or do instead: {/* REVIEW: Chris to confirm these are what he'd actually suggest. */}

- **Cheer for effort and for good ideas, after they happen.** "Great try!", "Nice idea!" or "I loved that pass" praise the child after the decision, so it doesn't compete with it.
- **Cheer for the team.** A clap and "Come on, team!" gives energy without telling one child what to do.
- **Use the name only for praise.** "Well done, Mia" is fine. "Go, Mia" can sound like a command.
- **Save the coaching for later.** Anything that sounds like instruction, like "shoot!" or "pass it!", is better left for me, or for a chat on the way home.
- **Say what they enjoyed.** Players in Knight's study wanted parents to cheer and praise good plays, and they wanted it most when the team was losing.

## One way to tell parents before the season

A short, friendly message in the first team email usually lands better than a correction on the touchline. Here's the kind of thing I'd send, which you can change to your own voice: {/* REVIEW: Chris to replace with his own wording. */}

> We're so glad your child is on the team. Saturdays are about the players learning to make their own decisions, and they're taking in a lot while they do it. One thing that helps them: cheer for effort and good ideas, and leave the instructions to me. Even a "Go!" can pull a player away from what they were about to do, because they hear it from the people they most want to please. If you'd like to help, "Great try!", "Nice idea!" and "Come on, team!" are perfect. Thanks for being a part of this.

## In the moment, and afterwards

Even with a good message, the first few games might still be loud. A few things I'd try: {/* REVIEW: Chris to confirm. These are suggestions from Chris's description of the problem, not tested methods. */}

- **Go first.** If you're calm and trust the players on the pitch, parents tend to follow.
- **Thank parents who do it well.** A word after the game to a parent who cheered for effort encourages the rest of them without singling anyone out.
- **Have a quiet word, not a speech.** If one parent keeps calling out instructions, a short chat after the game about that one behavior tends to work better than a message to the whole team.

## What I'd watch for

A parent who knows the game can sometimes help. One of the girls in Knight's study liked her dad giving her a single thing to work on before a game, as long as it matched what her coach was asking. That's a different thing from a running commentary, and it's worth being able to tell them apart.

## Common questions about parents coaching from the sideline

**Why is shouting "go" a problem for young soccer players?**
Young players are making many quick decisions, and a word like "go" from a parent can sound like an instruction. They may act on it even when the coach wants something else. That's what I see with younger players. I couldn't find a study that tests it directly.

**Do kids want their parents to coach from the sideline?**
In the one interview study I found, most didn't. Twenty-nine of 36 girls aged about 13 said parents shouldn't coach during games, mainly because it was confusing. It's a small group from one study, so I wouldn't treat it as the final word on every age.

**What should parents say instead at youth soccer games?**
Praise effort and good ideas after they happen, and cheer for the team. In the same study, players said they wanted clapping and praise, and wanted it most when the team was losing.

**How do I talk to parents about their sideline behavior?**
A short, friendly message before the season, with something to say instead, tends to work better than correcting people at a game. A private word after the game is better than calling someone out in front of others.

**Should coaches stay quiet on the touchline too?**
Less talking is worth trying. Studies of youth soccer coaching found instruction happening more than once a minute on average ([Cushion, Ford & Williams, 2012](https://doi.org/10.1080/02640414.2012.721930)), though those were practices, not matches.
