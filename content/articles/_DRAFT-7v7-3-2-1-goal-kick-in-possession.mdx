Building out is inviting the pressure in order to create space behind it.

That's how I think about a 7v7 goal kick. You play short, the other team steps up to take the invitation, and every player who steps up leaves space behind them. I coach it from a 3-2-1, and there's no set pattern: the players are solving problems of time, space and numbers.

## Who's where

I write shapes with outfield players only. A 3-2-1 is three defenders (one central, two wide), two central midfielders and one forward. With the keeper it's a 1-3-2-1.

In the US I see the 2-3-1 more than the 3-2-1, while European teams I've seen more commonly play the 3-2-1. That's an observation, not a count. At the international tournaments I've watched (strong U9 and U10 teams, and U11 where they play 7v7), the matchup I see most is a 3-2-1 against a 2-3-1. The 2-3-1 has two defenders and three midfielders, two of them wide. So the 3-2-1 controls the central channel more easily, and the 2-3-1 puts its width in midfield.

## The invitation

The central defender plays the goal kick short to the keeper, then checks wide. If the central defender were the one pressed, the keeper would be the only way out. I'd rather the keeper be pressed, with the central defender as the outlet.

<VideoClip clip="3-2-1-goal-kick-opening" title="The invitation" poster="/images/articles/3-2-1-goal-kick-opening-poster.png" caption="The central defender plays short to the keeper and checks wide. The opposition waits behind the build-out line. The clip holds as the ball is played." />

The risk is the keeper. At this age the keeper is rarely a full-time keeper, and teams that have one tend to be the stronger groups, where the keeper is a decent player anyway. If yours isn't comfortable with the ball, change the routine.

The build-out line buys the time. U.S. Soccer's standards put it 14 yards from goal from under-9, and the opposition has to stay behind it until the ball is back in play ([U.S. Soccer, 2017](https://www.ussoccer.com/stories/2017/08/five-things-to-know-how-smallsided-standards-will-change-youth-soccer)). Leagues word the release differently, so check yours. As I work from it, the press can't start until the first player passes or the second player receives, which usually gives the keeper time.

## Taking the invitation

Each clip picks up from the kick and holds the moment the opposition shows what it's doing. Ask the room at each freeze: what's the time, what's the space, what are the numbers? Whatever the answer, the job is the same. Break the first line with a pass or a dribble, establish possession, get faced forward, and enter their half.

### They press with three

<VideoClip clip="3-2-1-goal-kick-a-press-three" title="They press with three" poster="/images/articles/3-2-1-goal-kick-a-poster.png" caption="Three opposition players step up from the build-out line. The clip holds here." />

Three on the line, one behind, two at the back. Our first line is the keeper, the central defender and the two wide defenders: four against three, or three against three without the keeper, the way I count.

One way through is a pass into the middle behind them, another is a pass wide, another is a dribble by the central defender into the gap. It depends on what the front three do once the ball moves.

### They press with one

<VideoClip clip="3-2-1-goal-kick-b-press-one" title="They press with one" poster="/images/articles/3-2-1-goal-kick-b-poster.png" caption="One opposition player steps up to the keeper and the others hold their shape. The clip holds as the pressing player commits." />

The central defender and a wide defender are a 2v1 before the keeper is counted, and a 3v1 with the keeper. A single defender can't answer a 2v1: step to the ball and the pass goes, hold and the player on the ball has time. I run a 3-2-1 because it teaches players to ask the opposition questions like that, ones that can't be answered.

### They don't press

<VideoClip clip="3-2-1-goal-kick-c-no-press" title="They don't press" poster="/images/articles/3-2-1-goal-kick-c-poster.png" caption="The opposition holds its shape and doesn't step up. The central defender carries the ball forward." />

Rare. The central defender carries, and the team looks for numbers up further forward.

## Behind the press

Once the first line is broken, the players who pressed are behind the ball and the space they left is in front of it. If several pressed the keeper, we should be numbers up in their half. If not, establish possession and look for the forwards.

I count outfield players only. The overload is short because the pressers recover, and it's still the goal.

## What it costs

Width. Two central midfielders control the middle, which leaves the wide defenders to supply it, and the difficulty is getting them to support the attack. My rule of thumb is one wide player forward, usually on the ball side. For stronger teams in controlled possession it can look like a 1-4-1 if both move up, or a 1-2-3 if they go further.

## One thing to try

Pause your next 7v7 game right after a short goal kick and ask: who has time, where's the space, how many of us against how many of them? Then let it play on.

## Common questions about building out in 7v7

**Should a 7v7 team build out short from goal kicks?**
The one adult study I've found, from UEFA Euro 2020, linked long goal kicks to successful attacks and short ones to unsuccessful attacks across 595 goal kicks, and its authors expected the opposite ([Mendes, Rezende & Praça, *Human Movement*, 2023](https://doi.org/10.5114/hm.2023.125924)). It's men's tournament football, observed and not controlled, and I haven't found a youth equivalent. I build out short for what it lets me teach. If results are the aim, your answer may differ.

**What is the best formation for 7v7 at U9 and U10?**
There isn't one. A state association's summary of U.S. Soccer's standards lists 1-2-3-1 or 1-3-2-1 for under-9 and under-10 ([EPYSA](https://www.epysa.org/u-s-soccer-player-development-initiatives/)), the 2-3-1 and 3-2-1 with the keeper counted. This piece covers the 3-2-1.

**What's a build-out line?**
A line across the pitch 14 yards in front of each goal in U.S. Soccer's small-sided standards, from under-9. The opposition stays behind it while the keeper has the ball or a goal kick is taken, until the ball is back in play. Leagues vary.
