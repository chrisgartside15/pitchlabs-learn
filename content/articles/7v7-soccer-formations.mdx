<TLDR>
- Neither the 2-3-1 nor the 3-2-1 is "the" 7v7 formation. U.S. Soccer's 7v7 page shows the 3-2-1 as an example and says other formations may be used. The FA's 7v7 page shows both.
- In my opinion the 2-3-1 is the more balanced shape and the easier one for players to see. It has three clear lines, a defending pair, and a central midfielder with no partner to worry about.
- I run the 3-2-1. It grows out of the diamond we use at 4v4, and it lets me set problems the other team can't easily answer: 3v1s and 2v1s.
- No study I could find shows either shape produces better players or better results at this age. Choose for what you want your players to learn, and rotate them.
</TLDR>

Search for 7v7 formations and most pages will tell you the 2-3-1 is the standard. I went looking for who set that standard and couldn't find them.

U.S. Soccer's own 7v7 page shows one example shape, the 3-2-1, and says "these are examples and other formations may be used." The FA's 7v7 page shows both the 2-3-1 and the 3-2-1. It encourages coaches to let players try different positions. I couldn't find a federation that mandates either one. So the question for a coach isn't which one is correct. It's which one helps your players, and how you'd tell.

## Formation numbers explained: 2-3-1 or 1-2-3-1?

Formations here are written from defence to attack with the keeper left out, so every number adds up to six. A 2-3-1 is two defenders, three midfielders and one forward. A 3-2-1 is three, two and one.

The official documents count the keeper, so you'll see the same shapes written as 1-2-3-1 and 1-3-2-1. Same players, same shape. If a page or a league sheet seems to be recommending something different from what you read here, check which way it counts.

## What U.S. Soccer and the FA say about 7v7 formations

[U.S. Soccer's U9–U10 page](https://www.ussoccer.com/us-way/player-development/game/formations/u9-u10-7v7) shows the 3-2-1: one central defender and two wide defenders, two staggered midfielders, one forward. It says players "should be encouraged to try all positions at this age group." It also says each formation "builds on the previous format." Its 4v4 shape for the younger group is a diamond.

[The FA's 7v7 page](https://learn.englandfootball.com/articles-and-resources/coaching/resources/2025/How-to-play-and-manage-7v7-games) shows both shapes without describing roles, and suggests every player gets at least half of the game.

The ages differ. U.S. Soccer puts 7v7 at U9 and U10; the FA moves 7v7 to U10 and U11 from the 2026–27 season.

Other federations differ again. Italy's FIGC sets [7v7 for its Pulcini](https://files.figc.it/version/c:NmRlYWQ2MjktOGMxZC00:NjdlMDlhZDgtNGRmNi00/Allegato%207b%20-%20Approfondimento_Regole_U10-U11.pdf), the under-10s and under-11s, with 6v6 as a variant. Germany's DFB has its [E-Jugend play either 5v5 or 7v7](https://www.dfb.de/mehr-fussball/kinderfussball). The Dutch KNVB uses [6v6 up to under-10 and 8v8 for under-11 and under-12](https://www.knvb.nl/assist-scheidsrechters/scheidsrechtersco%C3%B6rdinator/wedstrijdvormen-pupillen). France plays [8v8 at under-10 and under-11](https://dfsm.fff.fr/simple/lois-du-jeu-football-danimation/), and Japan's JFA [recommends 8v8 for under-12s and younger](https://www.jfa.jp/documents/guideline/). None of the documents I read tells coaches which shape to play. Check what your own league plays and what rules come with it.

What actually gets played is a different question from what's published. In the U.S. I see the 2-3-1 more often. The European teams I've watched at international 7v7 tournaments more commonly play the 3-2-1. That's what I've seen, not a count, and I've watched those teams, not coached them.

## The 2-3-1 formation

<ImageLightbox src="/images/articles/7v7-formation-231.svg" alt="A 2-3-1 formation on a 7v7 pitch: the goalkeeper, two defenders, three midfielders (two wide and one central) and one forward." width="436" height="704" />
</div>

This is the one most coaches start with, and I understand why. In my opinion it's the more balanced shape, and the easier one for players to perceive.

It gives you three clear lines across the pitch. Every player can see who's in front of them and who's behind. The two defenders are a partnership, so each knows who they're covering and who's covering them. The wide midfielders typically play further forward, which gives you natural width without asking anyone to invent it. And the central midfielder doesn't have to think about a partner, so that player has one job to learn before learning the next.

For a group of new 7v7 players, that clarity matters: a player who can see their job doesn't have to work it out mid-game.

Where I see the 2-3-1 go wrong is when both wide midfielders are caught high up the pitch and the central midfielder then loses the ball. The wide players and the forward are already ahead of it, so only the two defenders are left behind it. The other team is suddenly running at them 3v2, and sometimes even 4v2, which is dangerous.

Coaching sites often add a second weak spot: the lone forward can end up isolated if the midfield doesn't push up. That's their view, not something I've measured.

## The 3-2-1 formation

<div className="formation-figure">
<ImageLightbox src="/images/articles/7v7-formation-321.svg" alt="A 3-2-1 formation on a 7v7 pitch: the goalkeeper, a central defender with a wide defender on each side, two central midfielders and one forward." width="436" height="704" />
</div>

I run the 3-2-1. It follows on from the diamond we look for at 4v4: a back player, two to the sides, one in front. Through the middle of the 3-2-1 the same diamond is still there, with a central defender, two central midfielders and a forward. Two wide defenders are added to it, so the shape grows from something the players already know.

The reason I coach it is the questions it lets me ask. A 3-2-1 builds 3v1s and 2v1s in front of the ball, and I teach players to ask questions that are close to impossible for the opponent to answer.

It also changes the matchup. Against a 2-3-1, which has two wide midfielders, the 3-2-1 has two central midfielders and finds it easier to control the middle channel. That's my coaching reasoning from how the shapes sit against each other, and not something I've seen measured.

It costs width. Two central midfielders hold the middle, so the wide defenders have to supply the width, and the real difficulty is getting them to support the attack. My own rule is to send one wide player forward at a time, usually on the ball side.

## The 3-1-2 formation

<div className="formation-figure">
<ImageLightbox src="/images/articles/7v7-formation-312.svg" alt="A 3-1-2 formation on a 7v7 pitch: the goalkeeper, a back three, one midfielder and two forwards." width="436" height="704" />
</div>

Three defenders, one midfielder and two forwards, written 1-3-1-2 when the keeper is counted. Put it next to the 3-2-1 and it's the same back three with one of the two central midfielders moved up beside the forward. The middle gets thinner and the front gets another player.

Neither U.S. Soccer nor the FA shows it as an example, and I couldn't find a federation that does. Coaching sites tend to call it the more attacking option, which is their view and not a finding.

The one piece of research on it is the 2025 study that set it against the 2-3-1. It used 18 sub-elite under-19 players in 7-a-side games, with keepers, on a 65 × 50 m pitch. Each bout lasted five minutes against the same 3-3 opponent. Compared with the 2-3-1, the 3-1-2 sat further back and narrower when attacking, and ran longer and more spread out when defending. That is where players stood, measured by GPS. It doesn't say which shape scored more, won more or taught more, and 19-year-olds on a bigger pitch aren't under-10s.

I don't have a view of my own on it to add, and I'd rather say that than borrow one.

## How to choose between a 2-3-1 and a 3-2-1

I wouldn't start from "which formation wins." I'd start from what I want players to learn this season and what I'd like them to see.

If the group is new to 7v7 and the main job is to understand lines, partnerships and width, the 2-3-1 gives that most simply. If the group has the basics and I want to teach them to create and solve numbers-up problems in the middle, the 3-2-1 gives me more to work with. It also asks more of the wide defenders.

It doesn't have to be one shape forever, either. Some of the highest-level teams are proficient in more than one, and on rare occasions they'll adapt to the opposition as needed.

Two things sit outside the shape:

- **Who plays where.** U.S. Soccer and the FA both say let players try every position. Football Australia's club game-time template goes further for its under-6 to under-11 groups: equal game time, and rotation through every position including goalkeeper. It's guidance, not a measured effect, so I'd hold it lightly. Still, a shape that suits your players in September may not in March, and rotating them gives you the information to change it.
- **Your keeper.** At this age the keeper is rarely a full-time keeper. A routine that starts from a short pass to the keeper works better if the keeper is comfortable with the ball.

## What the research says about 7v7 formations, and what it doesn't

I couldn't find a study that compares a 2-3-1 with a 3-2-1 at this age, or one showing that either produces better players or better results.

The nearest evidence is about the size of the game, not the shape. A Spanish study of 18 under-12 matches found goalkeepers passed short to nearby teammates more often in 7v7 and 8v8 than in 11v11 ([Sánchez-García & Sánchez-Sánchez, *RICYDE*, 2020](https://doi.org/10.5232/ricyde2020.06101)). Its authors say they know of no studies of how formation affects young players' technical and tactical demand. It's men's matches from one season in Spain, and it compares sizes of game, not shapes.

The closest thing to a shape comparison is a 2025 study of 18 under-19 players in 7-a-side games, which compared a 2-3-1 with a 3-1-2 against the same 3-3 opponent. The 2-3-1 was higher and wider in attack, and more compact in defence ([González-Rodenas et al., *Journal of Human Kinetics*, 2025](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC12127938/)). It measured where players stood, not who won, the players were 19, and it didn't include the 3-2-1.

So when a page tells you one of these shapes develops players better, it's a coach's opinion, including mine.

## A quick way to test both

Run the same group in one shape for two or three games and then in the other, and ask each player afterwards to tell you their job. The shape that's easier to describe is telling you something.

It's always important to ask the players where they feel most comfortable, too.

## Common questions about 7v7 formations

**What's the best formation for 7v7?**
There isn't a best one that I can show. U.S. Soccer gives the 3-2-1 as an example and says others may be used; the FA shows the 2-3-1 and the 3-2-1. No study I found compares them at this age.

**Which formation do U.S. Soccer and the FA recommend for 7v7?**
Neither mandates one. U.S. Soccer's page for under-9 and under-10 shows the 1-3-2-1 (the 3-2-1 here) as its example and says other formations may be used. The FA's 7v7 page shows both the 1-2-3-1 and the 1-3-2-1.

**Is the 2-3-1 the same as the 1-2-3-1?**
Yes. The 1-2-3-1 counts the keeper and the 2-3-1 doesn't.

**What about the 3-1-2?**
Neither U.S. Soccer nor the FA shows it as an example. It's the shape the one 2025 under-19 study I found compared against the 2-3-1. Coaching sites tend to call it the more attacking option, which is their view and not a finding. It's on the board so you can set it against the other two.

**What ages play 7v7?**
It depends on the country. U.S. Soccer uses U9 and U10, and the FA uses U10 and U11 from the 2026–27 season. Check your league.

**Should kids stay in one position?**
Both U.S. Soccer and the FA encourage trying all positions at this age. I haven't found a study that compares rotating with fixed positions at 8 to 10, so it's consensus and coaching practice and not a measured result.

**Is there a build-out line, and where does it go?**
Many leagues use one, and the distance varies more than most pages admit. U.S. Soccer's 2017 standards put it 14 yards in front of each goal; many U.S. league documents place it halfway between the top of the penalty area and the halfway line. Outside the U.S. it's often called a retreat line: some English county rules have the opposition retreat to halfway, and Scotland's 2026 7-a-side rules use 13 metres. The one thing the sources agree on is that the opposition stays back until the keeper releases the ball. Check your league's rules.
