Coaches stop the game more than they realize. Research that's tracked youth soccer coaching at every level, grassroots through academy, finds instruction happening more than once a minute during practice, and coaches' own guesses at their number run lower than what's actually recorded ([Cushion, Ford & Williams, *Journal of Sports Sciences*, 2012](https://doi.org/10.1080/02640414.2012.721930)). Most of us are bad judges of our own frequency. It's the same pattern this series has already found in how much coaches talk once they've decided to speak.

You've built the environment right. You've thought about what to say once you step in, and when to just stay quiet and let a player work it out. But there's a third decision underneath both of those, and most coaches never separate it out: how do you actually interrupt play to say anything at all? The cost isn't the same every time.

## The whole wheel, not just one ring

<DefinitionBox term="The Coaching Intervention Wheel">
A framework for the other half of environment — not what you build, but how and when you step into it. Three layers: who you're coaching, how you interact once you've decided to say something, and how you actually stop the game to say it.
</DefinitionBox>

The last two pieces in this series have already been using this wheel without naming the whole thing.

**At the center: who you're coaching.** Every intervention is aimed at one of four sizes of audience:

- **Individual** — one player.
- **Group** — players across units who share an area of the field, like a left back and the left winger in front of them.
- **Unit** — a full line of the team: defensive, midfield, or attacking.
- **Team** — everyone.

**The inner ring: how you interact, once you've stepped in.** Twelve ways to say something, from saying nothing at all to a direct command — Observe, Silence, Question and the rest of the menu from [the last piece in this series](/articles/how-much-should-you-actually-say), where coaches instructed roughly three times as often as they questioned.

**The outer ring: how you physically stop the game to say it.** Neither of the first two pieces got to this layer. It's what the rest of this piece is about.

## Six ways to stop a session, and what each one costs

Every way of stepping into a live session is one of six moves, and each has its own price tag in playing time:

- **Drive by.** One sentence, to one player, while everything else keeps moving. **Free.**
- **Drinks break.** Coach inside a pause you were already taking. **Free.**
- **Pull aside.** Take one or two players out while the rest keep playing. 20–30 seconds.
- **Freeze frame.** Everything stops, players hold their positions exactly where they are. 30–45 seconds.
- **Huddle.** Bring the whole group in, balls down. 60–90 seconds.
- **Walkthrough.** Rehearse the movement at walking pace, no pressure. 60–120 seconds.

Two of those six cost you nothing. Four of them spend playing time you don't get back. Naming them is what makes reaching for a huddle stop being the automatic move.

It's also, per the research above, the exact habit most coaches already have without realizing it. Instruction is the most frequent thing on the list, happening more than once a minute, and these six moves are how that instruction actually reaches the field.

## Time isn't the only cost

Each of these six also has a natural audience — which of the four groups above it actually reaches — and matching the stoppage to that audience matters as much as watching the clock. A drive-by fits an individual. A pull-aside fits a group or a unit. A huddle or a freeze frame reaches the whole team.

The mistake I see most often isn't picking an expensive option. It's picking the wrong *size* one. A coach spots one player drifting out of position, and instead of a five-second word on the move, the whole session stops for a huddle. Fifteen players wait for a message meant for one of them, and it costs them more than playing time — it's fifteen players' attention spent on something that wasn't for them, the same kind of noise [the first piece in this series](/articles/why-your-u8-practice-feels-like-chaos) covers in more depth. Match the stoppage to who actually needs to hear it.

## The two moves you should already be using constantly

**Drive by** costs nothing, so there's no reason to ration it. A single sentence to a single player, delivered without stopping anything, is the cheapest tool you have. Pair it with a quick Instruct, a Challenge, or a Reinforce and you've coached without spending a second of practice time.

**Drinks break** is free in the same sense, but with a catch: free in playing time isn't the same as free in attention. Players are resting, which means they're switched off in a way they aren't mid-rep. Use the moment, but keep it to one idea. Stack three coaching points into a thirty-second water break and you're doing the exact thing that overloads young players during live play — just standing still while you do it.

## When a bigger stoppage earns its cost

The other four aren't wrong to use. They're just not free, so each one should be doing something a cheaper option couldn't.

**Pull aside** earns its 20–30 seconds when the problem belongs to a couple of players specifically and the rest of the group doesn't need to hear it. It's the scaled-up version of a drive-by, still targeted, just needing a few more seconds to land.

**Freeze frame** is worth its 30–45 seconds when the exact picture, at this exact instant, is the whole teaching point — a shape of play, a spacing problem, a moment you want every player to actually see rather than have described to them after the fact. Used well, it's showing instead of telling.

Both are worth using deliberately rather than reflexively. Feedback delivered the instant a mistake happens can crowd out what the player would otherwise have noticed themselves, and lead to fixing something that didn't need fixing ([Wulf & Shea, in *Skill Acquisition in Sport*, 2004](https://gwulf.faculty.unlv.edu/wp-content/uploads/2014/05/Shea-and-Wulf-HMS-1999.pdf), cited via Cushion et al., 2012). A beat of silence before you freeze the picture or pull a player aside sometimes teaches more than the stoppage itself.

**Huddle** earns its 60–90 seconds when the message genuinely belongs to everyone: resetting the objective at the start of a phase, or regrouping after a run of chaos that needs a shared reset, not five individual corrections.

**Walkthrough** earns its 60–120 seconds when a movement needs to be seen at working speed once, without the pressure of live play, before it'll show up correctly when the game speeds back up.

These pairings aren't rules. Try one, watch what happens with your group, and adjust.

## Where this leaves you

STEP handles whether the environment itself needs to change. The inner ring of the Coaching Intervention Wheel handles what you say once you've decided to step in. This piece, the outer ring, handles how you physically interrupt to say it, and what that interruption costs. All three are one idea in practice — see [The Fundamentals of Soccer Coaching](/articles/fundamentals-of-soccer-coaching) for the version without the tools attached.

The same research base that found coaches over-instruct also found something about how sessions get built in the first place: youth sessions, across age and skill level, spend roughly two-thirds of their time in isolated drill practice and only about a third in small-sided, game-realistic play, with almost no change in that ratio even at nine years old ([Ford et al., 2010, cited via Cushion et al., 2012](https://doi.org/10.1080/02640414.2012.721930)). Left to habit, coaches default toward more structure and more control than the research says actually builds decision-makers.

Next session, keep count of your huddles. If most of what you said could have been a drive-by, that's where to start.

## Common questions about stopping a youth soccer session to coach

**How often should I stop a youth soccer practice to coach?**
Less often than feels natural. Two of the six ways to interrupt play, drive-by and drinks break, cost no playing time at all, and most coaches underuse both in favor of the whole-group huddle, which is the most expensive option on the list. Before reaching for a stoppage, ask whether a free one would do the same job.

**Do coaches really interrupt more than they realize?**
Studies that observed real practice sessions found coaches instructing more than once a minute on average, and found that coaches' own self-ratings of how often they do it line up with reality far less reliably than an outside observer's count ([Cushion, Ford & Williams, 2012](https://doi.org/10.1080/02640414.2012.721930)). It's not a discipline problem. It's just hard to track accurately from the inside of your own session.

**What's the difference between a freeze frame and a huddle?**
A freeze frame holds players exactly where they are on the field, so the specific spatial picture is the teaching point. A huddle brings everyone in with balls down, better suited to a message that isn't tied to a specific spot on the field. Freeze frame costs less time, 30–45 seconds against 60–90, but both stop the whole session, so neither is free.

**Is it bad to use a lot of drinks breaks to coach?**
Not in terms of playing time, since they're free. But players are resting and mentally checked out in a way they aren't during live play, so a drinks break is the wrong moment to stack multiple coaching points. Keep it to one idea, the same discipline that matters during live play too.
