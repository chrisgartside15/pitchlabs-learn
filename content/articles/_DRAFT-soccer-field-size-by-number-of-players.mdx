<TLDR>
- There's no official formula for field size, but published sizes follow a simple pattern: about 5.5 yards of width and 8.5 yards of length for every player on one team (5 x 8 in metres).
- For 7v7 that gives roughly 38 x 60 yards, within a couple of yards of the FA's and Germany's 7v7 fields and the size most US leagues use.
- Official fields give each player more room as players get older: about 100 square metres each at 5v5, 185 at 9v9 and 325 on an adult field.
- Research on small-sided games finds more space per player means more running and higher heart rates, and less space means more pressure. Shrink the numbers for a tighter game, stretch them for a more open one.
</TLDR>

Divide an FA 5v5 field by the ten players on it and each one gets about 100 square metres. A 9v9 field gives each player about 185. An adult 11v11 field gives each player about 325, more than three times what a seven-year-old gets. Official field sizes grow faster than the number of players on them, and that's a big part of why the same game feels so different from one format to the next.

Training fields are usually sized by eye: pace out something that looks about right, put the cones down, adjust when the game looks too crowded or too stretched. That works, but it means the space players get changes from one session to the next without anyone really deciding it. There's no official formula for field size. The published sizes do follow a pattern, though, and it's simple enough to do in your head at the field.

## The pattern: 5.5 yards wide, 8.5 yards long, per player

Take any federation's field size for a format and divide it by the number of players on one team. The FA's 7v7 field, 60 x 40 yards, works out at about 8.6 yards of length and 5.7 of width per player. Its 9v9 field comes out at 8.9 and 5.6. Germany's 7v7 field comes out at 8.6 and 5.5. Spain's and the common US 7v7 sizes land in the same place.

Round that off and you get a rule of thumb: **for every player on one team, about 5.5 yards of width and 8.5 yards of length.** In metres it's tidier still: 5 metres wide and 8 long.

No federation publishes this rule; it comes from dividing their sizes back down, which is why it gets you within a few yards of them. It keeps the field about one and a half times longer than it is wide, close to the shape of most real fields. It stops working at adult level, where fields jump to around 70 x 110 yards, but for youth formats it holds up well.

Queensland is the outlier, a little shorter and wider than the rest. Everyone else sits within a few yards of 60 x 40.

Formats change by country too. Spain plays fútbol 7 up to around U12, and in parts of Germany 7v7 is an exception to the normal U11 format rather than the standard. If your league plays a format that isn't in the table, the rule still works, because it only needs the number of players.

## What more space or less space does to the game

The research on this mostly measures area per player: the field's length times its width, divided by everyone on it. It's the same idea as the rule of thumb, just expressed in square metres.

When ten youth players played the same small-sided game on three field sizes, giving each player about 75, 175 and 275 square metres, the bigger fields produced higher heart rates, more distance covered and more high-intensity running, and players rated them as harder work ([Casamichana & Castellano, *Journal of Sports Sciences*, 2010](https://scinapse.io/papers/2071784257)). A larger study of 228 elite academy players aged U15 to U19 estimated that a small-sided game needs roughly 155 to 210 square metres per player to match the running demands of their official games, with sprinting needing the most space ([Riboli et al., *Biology of Sport*, 2021](https://doaj.org/article/9663fce2e5e842e0b5dc0bec2ed7d8ae)).

Both studies are older players than most of the coaches reading this work with, and both measure physical load, not touches or decisions. For what format size does to touches with younger players, see [Small-Sided Games for Young Players](/articles/small-sided-games).

What they do support is the general direction: shrink the space and you get more pressure and quicker decisions; open it up and you get more running and more time on the ball. In STEP terms, that's the Space lever, and [Why Your Soccer Practice Feels Like Chaos](/articles/why-your-practice-feels-like-chaos) covers how it fits with the other three.

So the rule of thumb is a starting point you can move either way:

- **Tighter, say 5 x 7.5 yards per player:** a 7v7 comes out at 35 x 52.5 yards, about a fifth less space than the rule. More pressure on the first touch and quicker decisions. Good for possession under pressure; frustrating if players can't keep the ball yet.
- **Around 5.5 x 8.5:** roughly the space players get in a match at that format.
- **Bigger:** more running, more time to look up, more room to attack in transition.

## Three things that change the numbers

**Goalkeepers.** The rule counts every player on the field, including keepers, and whether you count them changes a lot. One study of elite players found a game with goalkeepers needed about 187 square metres per player to match match-day running, against about 115 without them ([Riboli et al., *PLOS ONE*, 2020](https://air.unimi.it/handle/2434/766616)). If you're playing to end lines or small goals with no keepers, you can usually go a little smaller.

**Shape.** Two fields with the same area play differently. A wider field gives players room to spread out and switch play; a longer one gives more space to run forward and attack the goal. That's reasoning rather than a study, but it's why it's worth deciding which side you're changing rather than just making the field "bigger."

**Very small games.** Because the rule scales with the number of players, it shrinks quickly below 3v3. A 3v3 comes out at about 16.5 x 25.5 yards, in the same range as the 25 x 20 example in the STEP article. A 2v2 comes out at 11 x 17 yards and a 1v1 at 5.5 x 8.5, which is too small for most of what you'd want from those games. For those, size for what you want to happen first and use the rule as a check.

## Next time you set up

One thing to try: before the cones go down, count the players on one team and do the sum. Then decide whether you want this game tighter or more open than that, and move the cones on purpose. After ten minutes, look at whether you're seeing the pressure or the time on the ball you set the field up for.

## Common questions about field size

**How big should a 7v7 soccer field be?**
Most federations land around 60 x 40 yards, or 55 x 35–37 metres. A quick way to get there is 5.5 yards of width and 8.5 of length for each player on one team, which gives about 38 x 60 yards.

**Is there an official formula for field size?**
No. Federations publish a size for each format, and researchers use area per player (length times width, divided by the number of players). Dividing the published sizes by the number of players per team gives a rule of thumb that gets you close to both.

**How much space per player should a small-sided game have?**
It depends on what you want from the game. Studies with youth and academy players have used anywhere from about 75 to 275 square metres per player; more space meant more running and higher heart rates. Official youth fields work out at roughly 100 square metres per player at 5v5 and 185 at 9v9.

**Should I count goalkeepers when I size the field?**
Yes, the rule counts everyone on the field. If you play without goalkeepers, you can usually make the field a little smaller.

**Do field sizes differ outside the US?**
Not by much. Most countries publish sizes in metres, but for 7v7 they cluster around 55 x 35–40 metres, close to common US field sizes.
